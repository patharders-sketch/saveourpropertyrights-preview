Save your property rights. All of them. Including your neighbor's.

We love property rights. That's exactly why we take them seriously. Your rights are real, they are valuable, and they stop at the property line. A request to rezone farmland for thousands of homes isn't a property right. It's a request for new ones, paid for by everyone next door.

The oldest rule in property law

"Use your own property in such a way that you do not injure another's."

Sic utere tuo ut alienum non laedas. English and American courts have been applying it for centuries.

Property rights were never a license to do anything you want. From the start, they came with a built-in limit: your use of your land can't take away your neighbor's use of theirs.

That's why you can't dam the creek that feeds the farm downstream, pump the shared aquifer dry, or point a stadium light into the bedroom window across the road. Nuisance law came first. Zoning came later, as a way to settle those conflicts before the damage is done instead of in court after.

So when someone says "it's my land, I can do what I want," the honest answer is: you can, right up to the point where it becomes your neighbor's problem.

What you actually own

Ownership is a bundle of sticks. Every stick has fine print.

Lawyers describe property as a "bundle of rights." When you bought your land, you bought the bundle as it existed that day, including the zoning that came with it.

The stick you actually bought. Most undeveloped land in the county's Rural Area is zoned A-1, Agricultural, which allows one house per 10 acres. The 1998 plan that set the 10-acre minimum matched the zoning most of those parcels already had. A-1 has required 10-acre lots since 1982, so for most owners nothing was taken. That right is still there today, untouched.

Over the fence

Things that don't stay on your side of the property line

A thousand new rooftops on one parcel is not a private decision. These are the costs that cross the fence, and the people who pay them never got a vote at the closing table.

💧

Groundwater

Rural families drink from wells that share an aquifer. Pave the recharge area or draw it down, and the neighbor's well is the one that goes dry.

🌧️

Stormwater

Rain that used to soak into pasture now sheets off roofs and driveways and lands on whoever is downhill. Water always finds the neighbor.

🚗

Roads

Country roads were built for country traffic. Thousands of daily trips don't stay on the developer's land. They use your road, your intersection, your commute.

🏫

Schools & services

New homes need classrooms, fire coverage and ambulances. If the project doesn't pay for them, every taxpayer in the county does.

🌙

The night sky

Streetlights and parking lots don't stop at the property line either. Light trespass and skyglow turn a dark rural night into a permanent dusk for miles.

🏛️

Shared heritage

Battlefields, historic roads and scenic corridors belong to everyone. Some things, once paved, don't come back.

Myth vs. fact

Things you may have heard

Saying no to a rezoning takes nothing away. You keep every right you bought: the house, the farm, the ability to sell, the one-home-per-10-acres yield. A denied application leaves you exactly where you started.

What a rezoning does is add value by granting new density. Declining to give someone something new is not taking something they had.

You can, and nobody on this site says otherwise. Sell to a farmer, a family, a land trust, or a developer. The buyer simply gets the same bundle of rights you had. What you can't do is sell rights you don't own yet and expect the neighbors to make up the difference.

Prince William County now runs a voluntary Purchase of Development Rights (PDR) program. The County buys a parcel's residential development rights and records a permanent open-space easement. You keep the land, keep farming it, and get paid.

That's a property right in action: your choice, your compensation, nobody else's well paying for it.

The neighbor is a property owner too. Their well, their road, their view, their tax bill and their night sky are part of their bundle. Defending those things isn't anti-property. It's the most pro-property position there is.

If caring what happens next door makes someone a NIMBY, then property law has been NIMBY since the Middle Ages.

Residential growth brings revenue and it brings costs: schools, roads, public safety, water and sewer. Whether a project pays its own way depends on the specific application, its proffers and its infrastructure. Ask for the fiscal impact analysis. If nobody can show you one, that's your answer.

What Virginia law actually says

Zoning exists to protect property, not to punish it

Virginia law (Code of Virginia § 15.2-2283) says zoning ordinances exist to promote the health, safety and general welfare of the public. Among the purposes the General Assembly lists:

  • Providing adequate light, air and convenience of access
  • Protecting against overcrowding of land
  • Preventing undue density relative to available community facilities
  • Reducing danger and congestion in travel
  • Supporting adequate water, schools, parks and public safety

Read that list again. Every item protects someone's ability to enjoy what they own. Zoning is the neighbors' property rights, written down in advance.

"A nuisance may be merely a right thing in the wrong place, like a pig in the parlor instead of the barnyard."
U.S. Supreme Court, Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co. (1926), upholding local zoning

Nobody is against pigs. Nobody is against houses. The question is always: is this the right place? That question belongs to the whole community, not just the seller.

Get involved

Stand up for property rights. Yours and next door's.

1

Know the application

Look up the Comprehensive Plan Amendment or rezoning case number, read the staff report, and ask for the fiscal and traffic analysis.

2

Show up

Planning Commission and Board of County Supervisors hearings take public comment. Three minutes from a neighbor carries real weight. pwcva.gov

3

Explore PDR

If you own rural land and want to be paid for preserving it, look into the County's voluntary Purchase of Development Rights program.

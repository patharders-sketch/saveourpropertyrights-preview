Prince William County · Gainesville District · Item 12-D

A study is not a rezoning.

Item 12-D asks County staff to come back with a proposed study area, scope and public engagement plan for a heritage tourism small area plan. It does not change what anyone can do with their land today. Here are the facts, with sources, and the places where neighbors on both sides already agree.

What the directive actually does

Item 12-D is a two-page "proposed directive" from Supervisor George Stewart, on the Board's Tuesday, November 17 agenda at 2:00 PM ET. Read it yourself. It's short. Item 12-D (PDF)

"Supervisor Stewart would like to direct the County Executive to work with staff to review, evaluate, provide scope and boundaries, regarding a Small Area Plan for the Gainesville District ideally suited for Heritage Tourism, and to return with recommendations for the Board to consider initiating." Item 12-D, first paragraph
"Staff should return to the Board with a proposed study area, scope of work, community engagement process, timeline, and resource requirements necessary to undertake the Small Area Plan… The County Attorney's Office is directed to review the information and recommendations for any legal issues." Item 12-D, final paragraph

What it asks for

  • A staff recommendation on a study area, scope, timeline and community engagement process, which then comes back to the Board.
  • Protection for "wetlands, waterways, groundwater, and the Occoquan watershed," measures that "preserve dark skies, minimize impervious surfaces, noise and water pollution; conserve mature forests and tree canopies; and safeguard significant viewsheds."
  • Ways to "support existing property owners and businesses," "attract private investment," and strengthen farms and agritourism.
  • A market study that excludes data centers, coordinated with the County's tourism and agritourism studies.

What it does not do

  • It doesn't rezone any property or change any zoning rule.
  • It doesn't set a minimum lot size. There's no "40 acres" anywhere in it.
  • It doesn't adopt a plan. Staff report back, and the Board then decides whether to start one.
  • It doesn't jump the line. It tells staff to "place this Small Area Plan into the work program queue" behind three plans already underway.

They say, the facts

A new anonymous website urges a "no" vote on Item 12-D. People are free to oppose it, and many of their neighbors' concerns deserve a real answer. But several claims on that site don't match the public record. Here's each claim next to the source.

They say

"26,000 residents who could lose their property rights through this directive."

The facts

The directive changes no one's property rights. It asks for a staff study and a report back. The 26,000 figure is a count of everyone who lives inside one map scenario. That site's own map calls it an "illustrative wider scenario" (25,833 residents in the 2020 Census) and offers two other versions of the same area: 17,115 and 40,730. Living near a study area isn't losing a right.

Sources: Item 12-D text · their population map and methodology

They say

Stewart is trying to take property rights "under cover of darkness."

The facts

This item has been on the Board's public agenda three times. It was withdrawn on September 22 (then titled a "Rural Communities" small area plan), deferred on October 20, and is back as Item 12-D on November 17. The text is posted online, and the County's SpeakUp page is taking public comments on it right now. A directive isn't a public hearing, but any small area plan it leads to would need a town hall, a design charrette, and Planning Commission and Board hearings.

Sources: Sep 22 brief minutes (Item 11-J) · Oct 20 brief minutes (Item 12-A) · Nov 17 agenda · SpeakUp comments on 12-D · County small area plan process

They say

"Your land. Their plan": a 40-acre minimum parcel size and no public water and sewer.

The facts

Those lines come from page 31 of a July 10 citizen slide deck, and the slide is labeled "Example." Neither the 40-acre minimum nor the water and sewer limit appears anywhere in the directive the Board will consider. A brainstorming slide from July isn't the County's plan, and it isn't on Tuesday's agenda.

Sources: July 10 slide, p. 31 (as posted on their site) · Item 12-D text

They say

"Farm and forest designations frozen."

The facts

The August 10 brief they quote says the Agriculture and Forestry designations "in the current 2040 Comprehensive Plan should remain in place." That means keeping the plan the County already adopted. It isn't a new restriction. And that brief isn't the directive either.

Source: Aug 10 brief (as posted on their site)

They say

The directive brings "new regulations" to "restrict property development."

The facts

The directive asks staff to "consider recommendations for land use strategies and regulations… to manage future development." That sentence sits next to others asking staff to "support existing property owners and businesses" and "attract private investment." Recommendations are only recommendations. Under Virginia law, no zoning ordinance can be amended until it goes to the Planning Commission, and every amendment needs advertised public hearings.

Sources: Item 12-D text · Va. Code § 15.2-2285 · Va. Code § 15.2-2204

They say

"Special interest groups" wrote the directive and landowners were "shut out."

The facts

Residents, conservation groups and landowners all have the right to bring ideas to their supervisor. That's how local government is supposed to work, and the people behind that site are using the same right today. What counts is the text the Board actually votes on, which is public, and the process that follows. That process requires staff to propose a community engagement plan and the County Attorney to review it. We agree that landowners belong at the table from day one, and the directive already asks staff to propose a community engagement process.

Sources: Item 12-D text · County small area plan process

They say

The County's own planners had concerns.

The facts

Fair point. According to records posted on that site, Planning staff wrote that early drafts were "too prescriptive" and that one "predetermines the boundaries." That's a good reason for staff, not anyone else, to propose the boundaries and scope, which is exactly what the directive asks them to do before the Board decides anything more.

Sources: FOIA excerpts as posted on their site · Item 12-D text

They say

The lighting rules are credited to Patrick Harders, "who owns a lighting company."

The facts

True, and it's no secret. Patrick is a certified lighting designer and owns a lighting design business. A July citizen slide cites his framework on lighting historic sites well. The directive itself contains no lighting standard and no product requirement, and it names no person or company. It says only that protections should "preserve dark skies." Dark-sky lighting means less glare and light trespass: downlighting, shielded fixtures, and no light spilling onto your neighbor's property.

Sources: July 10 slide, p. 29 (as posted on their site) · Item 12-D text

They say

Patrick Harders was Supervisor Stewart's Republican opponent in 2025.

The facts

Also true. Patrick ran for Gainesville District Supervisor in 2025 and lost a close race to Supervisor Stewart. That's exactly why it should mean something that we agree on protecting western Prince William's trees, water, history and night skies. This isn't a party issue.

Where we agree

Strip away the labels, and most of western Prince William wants the same things. We'd welcome help from neighbors on every side of Item 12-D in protecting them.

Tree canopy

Keep mature forests and existing tree canopy. The directive asks to "conserve mature forests and tree canopies."

Preservation

Protect the battlefield setting, Thoroughfare, historic sites and rural character that make this place worth visiting and living in.

Dark skies

Sensible lighting: downlighting, shielded fixtures and no glare onto neighbors' property, so we can still see the stars.

Watershed

Protect wells, streams and the Occoquan watershed by limiting pavement and runoff.

Landowners at the table

Owners and residents should help shape any plan from day one. That's what a real community engagement process is for.

Community-led planning

Staff, not any one group, should draw the boundaries, in public, with neighbors in the room.

Property rights cut both ways

"Property rights" is the heart of this debate, so it's worth being precise about what they are.

What you own

Owning land gives you the right to use it under the zoning it has today. That's a real right, and we support it. A study or a small area plan doesn't take it away. A plan is guidance in the County's Comprehensive Plan, and changing what's allowed on a property is a separate step with its own public notice and hearings.

What a rezoning is

A rezoning application asks for rights the land doesn't have yet, such as many more houses or a data center where today's zoning doesn't allow one. Nobody is entitled to a rezoning. It's a decision the Board makes after Planning Commission review and public hearings, weighing what's best for the whole community.

Your neighbors have property rights too

Owning land has never meant you can harm the people next door with noise, light, runoff, traffic or contaminated wells. That idea is as old as property law itself, and it's built into Virginia's zoning law. Zoning exists to promote "the health, safety or general welfare of the public," and Virginia law requires reasonable consideration of "the existing use and character of property," "the conservation of natural resources," the "preservation of agricultural and forestal land," and "the conservation of properties and their values."

Protecting the rural area doesn't take anyone's rights away. It protects every owner's right to enjoy what they already have.

Sources: Va. Code § 15.2-2283 · § 15.2-2284 · § 15.2-2285. This is a general explanation, not legal advice.

What happens next

Even if the Board approves Item 12-D on Tuesday, every step after it is public.

  1. Tue, Nov 17 · 2:00 PM ET

    The Board considers the directive

    Item 12-D asks staff for a study proposal. Supervisors can approve, change, defer or reject it.

  2. If approved

    Staff report back

    Staff propose a study area, scope, community engagement process, timeline and cost, with legal review from the County Attorney. The plan waits in the queue behind the three already underway: Fettler Park, Vint Hill and Yorkshire.

  3. Board decision

    The Board decides whether to start a plan at all

    Nothing begins until the Board votes to initiate it.

  4. If initiated

    Neighbors shape it

    Under the County's published process: stakeholder meetings, a public town hall, a visioning and design charrette, then a draft.

  5. Before adoption

    Public hearings

    A Planning Commission and Board work session, then a Planning Commission public hearing and a Board of County Supervisors public hearing.

  6. Any zoning change

    A separate, noticed process

    Changing zoning on any property takes its own Planning Commission referral, advertised notice and public hearings under state law.

Sources: Item 12-D · County small area plan methodology (Tasks 1–7) · Va. Code § 15.2-2285
